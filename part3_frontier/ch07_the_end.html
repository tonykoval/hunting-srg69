Chapter 7 · Part III The End of the Hunt: Why $\mathrm{SRG}(69,20,7,5)$ Does Not Exist

The hunt is over, and the answer is no. It did not come from either of the two regions Chapter 5 left open: the involution sweep and the rigid Gram search were never finished. It came from a route that assumes no symmetry at all and never builds a graph. The $69$ vertices become vectors, the vectors lie in a lattice, and the lattices that could hold them can be listed completely.

Theorem 7.1 (2026-09-26)

There is no strongly regular graph with parameters $(69,20,7,5)$.

Corollary 7.2. There is no quasi-symmetric $2$-$(46,16,8)$ design with block intersection numbers $4$ and $6$. Its block graph, with two blocks adjacent when they meet in $4$ points, would be an $\mathrm{SRG}(69,20,7,5)$ (a short spectral argument, Appendix B, Corollary B.7).

Status of the proof

This is a computer-assisted proof. It was banked in the project on 2026-09-26 after two independent solver engines, two independent audits and a review of the write-up. The full write-up has every lemma proved, the data tables, and the complete source code in its appendices. It is docs/paper/srg69.tex (and srg69.pdf) in the orbit-gen repository. It is being prepared for review by a mathematician. It has not been published or peer-reviewed. This chapter is an outline in the book's own words. Where it and the write-up differ, the write-up is authoritative.

7.1   From a graph to $69$ vectors

Let $A$ be the adjacency matrix of a putative $\mathrm{SRG}(69,20,7,5)$. Its eigenvalues are $20$, $5$ and $-3$, with multiplicities $1$, $23$ and $45$ (Chapter 1). Shift by $3I$ and add the all-ones matrix $J$:

$\displaystyle G = 3I + A + J$

has eigenvalue $3+20+69=92$ on the all-ones vector, $3+5=8$ with multiplicity $23$, and $3-3=0$ with multiplicity $45$. So $G$ is positive semidefinite of rank $24$. It is therefore the Gram matrix of $69$ vectors $v_1,\dots,v_{69}$ spanning $\mathbb{R}^{24}$, with

$\displaystyle |v_i|^2 = 4,\qquad \langle v_i,v_j\rangle = \begin{cases} 2 & \text{if } i\sim j,\\ 1 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$

Compare Chapter 4, which used the $5$-eigenspace alone (dimension $23$, real coordinates). Adding $J$ costs one dimension and makes every inner product an integer. The vertices then span an even lattice $L_X$ of rank $24$, the vertex lattice. We call a family of $69$ vectors with this Gram matrix a configuration, and any lattice containing one a host. The theorem says that no configuration exists.

Two facts about $L_X$ drive everything. Let $s=\sum_i v_i$. Every vertex pairs $4 + 2\cdot 20 + 1\cdot 48 = 92$ with $s$. The second-moment operator $M=\sum_i v_iv_i^{\mathsf T}$ has the same nonzero spectrum as $G$, which pins it down completely:

$\displaystyle M = 8I + 7\,uu^{\mathsf T},\qquad u = s/23,\qquad |u|^2 = 12,\qquad \langle v_i,u\rangle = 4 .$

The first surprise: $u$ lies in $L_X$. For a vertex $v$, $Mv=\sum_j\langle v_j,v\rangle v_j$ is an integer combination of vertices, and it also equals $8v+28u$. So $28u\in L_X$. Since $23u=s\in L_X$ and $\gcd(28,23)=1$, $u\in L_X$. The same computation applied to a dual vector shows $8L_X^{\#}\subseteq L_X$, so $\det L_X$ is a power of $2$.

The moment identities (the workhorse)

Let $N$ be any lattice containing $L_X$, and $x$ a vector of its dual $N^{\#}$. Then the pairings $c_i=\langle x,v_i\rangle$ are integers, and with $h=\langle x,u\rangle$:

$\displaystyle \sum_i c_i = 23h,\qquad \sum_i c_i^2 = 8|x|^2 + 7h^2,\qquad (Ac)_i = 5c_i + 5h .$

Summing $c(c\mp1)\ge 0$ over the $69$ integers gives the inequality $8|x|^2+7h^2\ge 23|h|$. A single short dual vector that violates it rules out a host outright. Later this one line closes thousands of cases without any search.

7.2   Only two kinds of host

Enlarge $L_X$ to a maximal even overlattice $N$. It still contains the vertices and $u$. Maximality forces the discriminant form $q_N$ on $D(N)=N^{\#}/N$ to be anisotropic: otherwise an isotropic class could be adjoined. Since $8N^{\#}\subseteq N$, $D(N)$ is a $2$-group, and anisotropy forces its exponent down to at most $4$. An exact enumeration of the anisotropic forms on small $2$-groups, re-done by an independent brute force, then combines with Milgram's formula. The formula says the Gauss-sum signature of $q_N$ equals the rank, $24\equiv 0 \pmod 8$. Only two cases survive:

The dichotomy

Either $N$ is even unimodular of rank $24$, that is, one of the $24$ Niemeier lattices (the Leech lattice $\Lambda$ or one of the $23$ with roots). Or $\det N = 8$ with discriminant form $\langle 7/4\rangle\oplus\langle 1/2\rangle$. All lattices of the second kind form a single genus $\mathcal{G}_8$ (Nikulin, 1980).

One more hand lemma is used everywhere below. For a root $r$ (a norm-$2$ vector) of a host that contains $u$, put $h=\langle r,u\rangle$. Then $h\in\{0,\pm1\}$. If $h=0$, every $\langle r,v_i\rangle\in\{0,\pm1\}$. If $h=\pm1$, every $\langle r,v_i\rangle\in\{0,h\}$. For instance, $h=2$ would need $\sum c_i^2 = 44$ and $\sum c_i = 46$, so $\sum c_i(c_i-1) = -2 < 0$.

7.3   The Leech lattice

If $N=\Lambda$, then $u$ is a norm-$12$ vector of the Leech lattice. The norm-$12$ vectors fall into exactly two orbits of its automorphism group $\mathrm{Co}_0$, and this is proved by counting:

  • Orbit $\mathcal{A}$, of $(2^{24})/\sqrt8$. Its stabiliser has order $|M_{24}|=244{,}823{,}040$, confirmed by a nauty computation on the $759$ octad vectors. So $|\mathcal{A}| = |\mathrm{Co}_0|/|M_{24}| = 33{,}965{,}568{,}000$.
  • Set $\mathcal{B}$, the sums $v_1+v_2$ of two minimal vectors at inner product $2$. The pair is determined by the sum, so $|\mathcal{B}| = 196{,}560\cdot 4{,}600/2 = 452{,}088{,}000$.
  • Together these are $34{,}417{,}656{,}000$ vectors, exactly the number of norm-$12$ vectors given by the theta series of $\Lambda$.

Case $\mathcal{A}$. The only minimal vectors that pair $4$ with $u$ are the $759$ vectors supported on the octads of the Golay code. So the $69$ vertices would be $69$ octads, meeting pairwise in $2$ or $4$ points. The moment identity $M=8I+7uu^{\mathsf T}$ then says that every point lies in $23$ of them and every pair of points in $7$. That is a quasi-symmetric $2$-$(24,8,7)$ design made of octads, and Brouwer and Calderbank (1986) proved that no such family exists.

Case $\mathcal{B}$. Write $u=v_1+v_2$. Every candidate vertex must pair $2$ with both $v_1$ and $v_2$, so it lies in the hyperplane $(v_1-v_2)^{\perp}$. The vertices span $\mathbb{R}^{24}$, which is a contradiction. (The candidate pool has $891$ vectors, of rank $23$.)

The octad case is the one place where the proof leans on a published theorem rather than on this project's own code. A direct computer check of the same octad pool by both engines was started to remove that dependency. It had not finished when this chapter was written, so the proof uses the published theorem.

7.4   The $23$ Niemeier lattices with roots

Each of the $23$ lattices was built from its glue code and checked to be even, of determinant $1$, and to have exactly $24h$ roots ($h$ the Coxeter number). Niemeier's classification then identifies it uniquely. The Weyl group acts on each lattice, so $u$ may be taken dominant. The host-root lemma then says that $u$ pairs $0$ or $1$ with every positive root. The enumeration used the weaker, still sound, condition $\{0,1,3\}$. It lists $4{,}211$ candidates for $u$ in $17$ of the lattices. The other six ($E_8^3$, $D_{16}E_8$, $D_{24}$, $A_{24}$, $A_{17}E_7$, $A_{12}^2$) admit none.

For each candidate, the vertices must lie in the pool $P_u=\{v\in N : |v|^2=4,\ \langle v,u\rangle=4\}$. Two exact certificates were checked:

  • Rank. The vertices span $\mathbb{R}^{24}$, so the pool must have rank $24$. A failure is certified by an integer kernel vector, checked in integers. This kills $707$ candidates.
  • Quadric relations. If a quadric vanishes on every pool vector, then summing it over the $69$ vertices gives an identity that involves only $M$ and $u$, both known. A nonzero value of that identity is a certificate. This kills $3{,}502$ candidates.

That leaves $4{,}211 - 707 - 3{,}502 = 2$ survivors, both in $A_1^{24}$. One pairs $3$ with a root, which the host-root lemma forbids. The other has exactly the octad pool of §7.3 as its pool, which is excluded there.

7.5   The determinant-$8$ genus

A complete catalogue. Take a lattice of $\mathcal{G}_8$ and add a root $t$ orthogonal to it. The sum has a unique odd unimodular overlattice $U$, of dimension $25$. This gives a bijection between the lattices of $\mathcal{G}_8$ and pairs $(U,t)$ of such a lattice and a root in it. Borcherds classified all $665$ odd unimodular lattices of dimension $25$. Taking one root per root-system component of each gives $4{,}212$ catalogue hosts. Completeness is certified by a mass identity. The sum of $\#\mathrm{Roots}(U)/|\mathrm{Aut}(U)|$ over Borcherds' table equals, as an exact rational number, the mass of $\mathcal{G}_8$ computed independently with Hecke:

$\displaystyle \mathrm{mass}(\mathcal{G}_8) = \frac{58611124254894503831340908639}{608048902695721102473166848000000} \approx 9.639\cdot 10^{-5}.$

The identity can only certify completeness if the $665$ rows are pairwise non-isometric, and that was checked separately.

The reduction $u = 2z$. A configuration in any lattice of $\mathcal{G}_8$ can be moved, by an explicit two-neighbour step, into a catalogue host in which $u=2z$ with $z\in K=U\cap t^{\perp}$ and $|z|^2=3$. Three short lemmas, all proved by hand from the moment identities, then constrain $z$:

  • $z$ is orthogonal to every root and every unit (norm-$1$ vector) of $U$;
  • if $K$ contains a unit, the host contains no configuration with $u=2z$ (the unit lemma);
  • the root components of the host must satisfy counting inequalities (the row rules). In particular the root system has rank at most $23$.

Pools and screens. Given $z$, every vertex has the form $v=w+z$ with $w\in K$, $|w|^2=3$ and $\langle w,z\rangle=-1$. So the pool of candidate vertices is read off from the norm-$3$ shell of $K$. Each pool was screened by rank, by moment membership modulo $p$ for $p\in\{2,3,5,65521\}$ (every certificate re-checked in integers), and by quadric relations. 7.6   The last six classes

What remains is a finite set-selection problem. In a fixed host, a configuration is a subset $X$ of the pool with $|X|=69$, inner products in $\{1,2\}$, exactly $20$ partners at inner product $2$ for each member, and $\sum X = 23u$. On top of that come constraints that hold for every configuration in that host: the moment identities for every pool vector, for every host root and for every short dual vector, and the $\lambda/\mu$ conditions around a pinned vertex. Adding a constraint that every real configuration satisfies cannot lose one. An infeasible model is therefore a proof that the pool holds none.

Two pools with the same Gram matrix hold the same configurations, so one representative per Gram-isomorphism class suffices. All $1{,}815$ non-representative memberships of the six classes were verified as explicit isomorphisms. Where a whole-pool run did not finish, the pool's own symmetry group, computed by nauty, split the search into sound cases: "contains the representative of orbit $j$ and avoids orbits $1,\dots,j-1$".

7.8   How it was checked

A search that reports "nothing" is only as good as its ability to find what is there. This book has told that story several times (§5.1, the fire-drill of Chapter 5). No $\mathrm{SRG}(69,20,7,5)$ exists to plant, so the controls are known graphs of the same shape: Gram matrix $3I+A+J$, norms $4$, inner products in $\{1,2\}$. Each is placed in its own lattice, with decoy vectors where applicable, and the engines must recover it.

  • Must-find controls. $\mathrm{SRG}(36,15,6,6)$ (a Latin-square graph) was found by both engines. The Paulus graphs $\mathrm{SRG}(26,10,3,4)$, in pools of $252$ and $572$ vectors with $226$ and $546$ decoys, were found by both engines. $\mathrm{SRG}(64,45,32,30)$, in hosts with $12$ and $36$ roots orthogonal to $u$, was found by engine 1 with the root identities switched on. Engine 2's certificates fire on none of the controls.
  • A second engine. Engine 2 was written independently of engine 1. It shares no code with it and derives its model from $(69,20,7,5)$ alone. It is a pure CNF with exact totalizer counters, solved by kissat 4.0.4. Every UNSAT comes with a DRAT proof checked by drat-trim. PARI only proposes short dual vectors; every one is re-checked exactly before use.
  • Two audits. An adversarial audit re-derived the reduction steps. An end-to-end audit then re-screened $40$ hosts from scratch with code that imports nothing from the original screen, and every kill and every survivor agreed. It also rebuilt all $4{,}212$ hosts with Hecke and matched their root, unit and $z$ counts, verified $736$ class memberships as explicit isomorphisms, and recomputed the orbit decompositions from verified generators.
  • A proof review. An independent reader re-derived every lemma of the write-up. It found no error that breaks the proof, and its corrections are applied in the write-up. It checked the Leech counting argument, verified all $1{,}815$ class memberships, and showed that the Borcherds rows sharing a root system ($16$ groups, $33$ rows) are pairwise non-isometric.
  • A certifier. A final script checks that every host has a verdict and every surviving pool belongs to a class. It also checks that every class has an INFEASIBLE verdict, from a whole-pool run or from orbit cases that are all INFEASIBLE and whose sizes sum to the pool. It rejects partial and MILP lines, and it printed CERTIFICATE: PASS.
What rests on less than the rest

The write-up names these explicitly. The external inputs are three: the order of $\mathrm{Co}_0$, the count $4600$ and the theta series of $\Lambda$; Borcherds' table of the $665$ lattices with their automorphism orders, cross-checked by the mass identity; and Brouwer–Calderbank for the octad pool. Two computations rest on a single implementation. One is the enumeration of candidate $u$ in the Niemeier lattices: each kill is certified individually, but the completeness of the candidate list rests on one reviewed program. The other is the use of Borcherds' list itself.

7.9   What this does to the rest of the book

Chapters 1–6 were written while the question was open. They are kept unchanged as the record of the hunt, each with a note at the top. Every partial result in them is now a consequence of Theorem 7.1, because a graph that does not exist has no automorphisms. That covers the orders $5$, $23$ and every prime $\ge 7$, the Cayley closure, the order-$3$ re-derivation, the involution computations and the rigid Gram wall. None of them is used in the proof. None of the campaigns they describe needs to be finished.

Three lessons carry over.

  • The iron law was about searching for graphs. Every method in Chapters 2–4 searched a space of graphs, with or without a prescribed symmetry, and each hit the wall the series describes. The ending counted lattices instead. Lattices of the right rank and determinant are finite in number and classified, so the search space was finite from the start. This does not refute the law. It goes around it.
  • The discipline did transfer. Must-find controls on known graphs of the same shape, two engines with no shared code, checkable UNSAT certificates, ledgers tallied by script, and the rule that a timeout proves nothing are the same habits this book recorded in Chapter 5. They are what the proof's credibility rests on.
  • The method travels, with care. The same idea applied to $\mathrm{srg}(99,42,21,15)$ is recorded in the sister volume Hunting the Unfinished. There it has produced a candidate nonexistence theorem, whose write-up is under review. It is not claimed here.
