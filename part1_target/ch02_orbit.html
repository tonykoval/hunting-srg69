Chapter 2 · Part I The Orbit-Matrix Reduction

Searching $2^{\binom{69}{2}} = 2^{2346}$ graphs directly is hopeless. The move that makes the problem tractable — for the symmetric part of it — is to assume a symmetry and quotient by it. A prescribed automorphism collapses the $69\times69$ adjacency matrix to a tiny orbit matrix, which can be enumerated exhaustively.

2.1   Prescribed automorphisms

Suppose $\Gamma$ is an $\mathrm{SRG}(69,20,7,5)$ with an automorphism $\sigma$ of prime order $p$. Then $\sigma$ partitions the $69$ vertices into $f$ fixed points and $\tfrac{69-f}{p}$ orbits of size $p$. Since orbits have size $p$, $\;f \equiv 69 \pmod p$. The adjacency matrix, written in this orbit basis, becomes constant on orbit-blocks — its data is captured by a $b \times b$ orbit matrix with $b = f + \tfrac{69-f}{p}$ rows, far smaller than $69$.

A nontrivial automorphism cannot fix too much: for an SRG with smallest eigenvalue $s$, $\;\mathrm{fix}(\sigma) \le \tfrac{\max(\lambda,\mu)}{\,k-r\,}\,n$. For $(69,20,7,5)$, $r=5$, so $\mathrm{fix} \le \tfrac{7}{15}\cdot 69 = 32.2$, i.e. at most $32$ fixed points. Combined with $f\equiv 69 \pmod p$ this leaves only a short list of $(p,f)$ to check.

2.2   The column orbit matrix

Definition (column orbit matrix; Behbahani–Lam)

For an automorphism with orbit-length distribution $(n_1,\dots,n_b)$, a $b\times b$ integer matrix $C=[c_{ij}]$ is a column orbit matrix of an $\mathrm{SRG}(v,k,\lambda,\mu)$ if

$\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{b} c_{ij} = \sum_{j=1}^{b}\frac{n_j}{n_i}c_{ij}=k, \qquad \sum_{s=1}^{b}\frac{n_s}{n_j}c_{is}c_{js}=\delta_{ij}(k-\mu)+\mu n_i+(\lambda-\mu)c_{ij},$

with $0\le c_{ij}\le n_i$ and $0\le c_{ii}\le n_i-1$. The entry $c_{ij}$ counts neighbours an orbit-$i$ representative has in orbit $j$. Every $\mathrm{SRG}$ with the prescribed automorphism yields such a matrix; conversely each $C$ is a necessary scaffold to be lifted back to a full graph.

The point is the size: for $(69,20,7,5)$ with $p=3$, $b$ ranges over $16$–$23$; with $p=5$, $b$ is $12$–$13$. Enumerating integer matrices that small — under the two equations above, plus canonical-form dedup to kill relabellings — is a finite computation a desktop can finish.

2.3   Two outcomes, both decisive

For each $(p,f)$ the orbit-matrix enumeration ends in one of two ways, and both advance the problem:

  • Zero orbit matrices. No $\mathrm{SRG}(69,20,7,5)$ admits an automorphism of order $p$ with $f$ fixed points. Sweep all $f$ and all $p$, and you have proved no graph has any nontrivial automorphism — a clean, verifiable theorem.
  • One or more orbit matrices. Each is a candidate skeleton. Lift it — fill in the within-orbit circulant blocks consistently — and any successful lift is a genuine $\mathrm{SRG}(69,20,7,5)$: existence, settled.
The pipeline

prescribe $(p,f)$  →  enumerate orbit matrices  →  (none ⇒ rule out)  |  (some ⇒ lift)  →  valid graph?

Chapter 3 reports what this engine has actually returned across every prime order — a sweep that has already closed all of $p=3$, $p=5$, the larger primes, and the striking order-$23$ case.
