Appendix B Theorems & Proofs

The verifiable results of the hunt, collected. Each "no automorphism" and "no Cayley" statement is an exhaustive computation that terminated in a real certificate, not a budget.

How to read this appendix after 2026-09-26

Theorem B.6 settles the question: no $\mathrm{SRG}(69,20,7,5)$ exists (Chapter 7; computer-assisted proof, write-up under review). Results B.3–B.5 were proved while the question was open. They are kept as history and are now consequences of B.6. B.1 and B.2 are general facts and remain in use.

Theorem B.1 (spectrum) — Ch 1

Every $\mathrm{SRG}(69,20,7,5)$ has adjacency spectrum $\{20^1, 5^{23}, (-3)^{45}\}$.

Proof. The non-principal eigenvalues solve $x^2-(\lambda-\mu)x-(k-\mu)=x^2-2x-15=0$, giving $r=5,\,s=-3$. Multiplicities follow from $1+f+g=69$ and $20+5f-3g=\mathrm{tr}(A)=0$: $f=23,\,g=45$. Both integral, so the parameter set passes the integrality condition. $\square$

Lemma B.2 (fixed-point bound) — Ch 2

A nontrivial automorphism $\sigma$ of an $\mathrm{SRG}(69,20,7,5)$ fixes at most $32$ vertices, and its fixed-point count satisfies $f\equiv 69 \pmod{\mathrm{ord}(\sigma)}$.

Proof. $\mathrm{fix}(\sigma)\le \frac{\max(\lambda,\mu)}{k-r}\,n=\frac{7}{15}\cdot 69=32.2$ (a standard eigenvalue bound). Non-fixed vertices lie in orbits of size $\mathrm{ord}(\sigma)$, so $69-f\equiv 0$. $\square$

Theorem B.3 (no odd-order automorphism) — Ch 3

No $\mathrm{SRG}(69,20,7,5)$ admits an automorphism of order $3$, $5$, or $23$ (hence of any odd prime, the larger primes returning $0$).

Proof. Exhaustive orbit-matrix enumeration (Behbahani–Lam) for $p=3$ over all admissible $f$ (orbit counts $16$–$23$, up to $1{,}177{,}968$ raw cubes) and $p=5$ ($f=4,9$) returns $0$ matrices in every case; the prescribed-$\mathbb{Z}_{23}$ existence SAT instance is UNSAT (kissat, $23$ s). Each "0 matrices" is an EXHAUSTED certificate, so no graph carries the prescribed symmetry. $\square$

Erratum and status (2026-09-26). The order-$3$ part of this statement was downgraded in Chapter 3 (2026-08-21) to a re-derivation in progress, because the enumeration behind it predates the audited generator. The parenthetical "(Behbahani–Lam)" names the method. Their published runs start at $p=5$ and do not cover $p=3$ (§5.1). The re-derivation was never finished. The whole statement, order $3$ included, now follows from Theorem B.6.

Theorem B.4 (no Cayley graph) — Ch 4

No $\mathrm{SRG}(69,20,7,5)$ is a Cayley graph.

Proof. $69=3\cdot 23$ with $\gcd(3,22)=\gcd(23,2)=1$, so the only group of order $69$ is $\mathbb{Z}_{69}$. A Cayley $\mathrm{SRG}$ on $\mathbb{Z}_{69}$ requires a $(69,20,7,5)$ partial difference set; an exhaustive/high-effort PDS search (annealing, $500\text{k}$ restarts; cyclic-Cayley SAT closure UNSAT in $9$ s) finds none. $\square$

Corollary B.5 (standing structure) — Ch 5

Any $\mathrm{SRG}(69,20,7,5)$, if it exists, has automorphism group a $2$-group and is not a Cayley graph; once the order-$2$ orbit-matrix sweep closes, its automorphism group is trivial. The two open regions are therefore the involution sweep ($p=2$) and the fully rigid case, both decided by the sound Gram-PSD / orbit-matrix enumerations of Chapters 3–4. $\square$

Superseded (2026-09-26) by Theorem B.6. Neither open region was finished. Neither is needed.

Theorem B.6 (nonexistence, 2026-09-26) — Ch 7

There is no strongly regular graph with parameters $(69,20,7,5)$.

Proof outline. The matrix $G=3I+A+J$ is the Gram matrix of $69$ norm-$4$ vectors spanning an even lattice $L_X$ of rank $24$ that contains $u=s/23$ (Lemma B.8). A maximal even overlattice $N$ of $L_X$ is either a Niemeier lattice or lies in one genus $\mathcal{G}_8$ of determinant $8$. The Leech lattice is excluded by a counting argument and by Brouwer–Calderbank (1986) for the octad case. The $23$ Niemeier lattices with roots are excluded by exact certificates for all $4{,}211$ candidates for $u$. For $\mathcal{G}_8$, a complete catalogue of $4{,}212$ hosts is built from Borcherds' $665$ odd unimodular lattices of dimension $25$, and its completeness is certified by an exact mass identity. Hand lemmas and exact screens reduce this case to $10{,}183$ candidate pools. $8{,}362$ of them die by the moment inequality in their own host. The remaining $1{,}821$ form six Gram-isomorphism classes, each shown infeasible by two independent engines (CP-SAT, and CNF/kissat with DRAT proofs checked by drat-trim). Details, verification and external inputs are in Chapter 7. $\square$

Status. This is a computer-assisted proof, banked in the project on 2026-09-26 after two independent audits and a review. The full write-up is docs/paper/srg69.tex in the orbit-gen repository. It is being prepared for review by a mathematician and has not been published or peer-reviewed.

Corollary B.7 (no quasi-symmetric design) — Ch 7

There is no quasi-symmetric $2$-$(46,16,8)$ design with block intersection numbers $4$ and $6$.

Proof. Such a design has $r=24$ and $b=69$ blocks. Let $N$ be its $46\times 69$ incidence matrix and $A$ the adjacency matrix of the block graph, with blocks adjacent when they meet in $4$ points. Then $NN^{\mathsf T}=16I+8J$ and $N^{\mathsf T}N=16I+4A+6(J-I-A)=10I-2A+6J$. The nonzero spectrum of $N^{\mathsf T}N$ is that of $NN^{\mathsf T}$: $384$ once and $16$ with multiplicity $45$. The eigenvalue $0$ has multiplicity $23$. On the all-ones vector, $10-2k+414=384$ gives $k=20$. On its complement, $10-2\theta\in\{16,0\}$ gives eigenvalues $-3$ (multiplicity $45$) and $5$ (multiplicity $23$). A connected regular graph with exactly three distinct eigenvalues is strongly regular. With $\theta_1=5$ and $\theta_2=-3$ it has $\mu=k+\theta_1\theta_2=5$ and $\lambda=\mu+\theta_1+\theta_2=7$. So the block graph would be an $\mathrm{SRG}(69,20,7,5)$, contradicting Theorem B.6. $\square$

Lemma B.8 (the vertex lattice) — Ch 7

Let $v_1,\dots,v_{69}\in\mathbb{R}^{24}$ have Gram matrix $3I+A+J$ for an $\mathrm{SRG}(69,20,7,5)$, let $L_X$ be the lattice they span and $s=\sum_i v_i$. Then $u=s/23\in L_X$, $|u|^2=12$, $\langle v_i,u\rangle=4$, and $M=\sum_i v_iv_i^{\mathsf T}=8I+7uu^{\mathsf T}$. Moreover, for any lattice $N\supseteq L_X$ and any $x\in N^{\#}$, the integers $c_i=\langle x,v_i\rangle$ and $h=\langle x,u\rangle$ satisfy $\sum_i c_i=23h$, $\sum_i c_i^2=8|x|^2+7h^2$ and $8|x|^2+7h^2\ge 23|h|$.

Proof. $G$ has eigenvalues $92$ (on $\mathbf{1}$), $8$ (multiplicity $23$) and $0$, and $M$ has the same nonzero spectrum. Since $\langle v_i,s\rangle=4+40+48=92$, $s$ is the $92$-eigenvector of $M$ with $|s|^2=69\cdot 92=12\cdot 23^2$, so $M=8I+\tfrac{7}{529}ss^{\mathsf T}$. For a vertex $v$, $Mv=\sum_j\langle v_j,v\rangle v_j\in L_X$ and $Mv=8v+\tfrac{28}{23}s$, so $\tfrac{28}{23}s\in L_X$. Together with $s\in L_X$ and $\gcd(28,23)=1$ this gives $s/23\in L_X$. The moment identities are $\sum_i c_i=\langle x,s\rangle$ and $\sum_i c_i^2=x^{\mathsf T}Mx$. The inequality follows by summing $c_i(c_i\mp1)\ge 0$. $\square$
